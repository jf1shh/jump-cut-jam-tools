Research date: 2026-10-27/28. 26 sources fetched (6 search queries returned no extractable claims from their own call and are omitted below; 20 fetched pages produced claims), ~1,200 words of quotes extracted. **This is a reconstructed synthesis, not a fully adversarially-verified report.** The original `/deep-research` run (task `w5gzpo14k`) completed its Fetch phase — every claim below comes from that phase's raw extractions — but the orchestrating session was interrupted before the Verify and Synthesize phases ran. ## 1. What jam judges actually reward

### Takeaway
Across three independent commentary sources (two GMTK postmortem roundups and one jam survival guide), the same pattern repeats: finished-and-simple beats ambitious-and-incomplete, and *how* a theme is interpreted matters more than how impressively a game is built. This maps cleanly onto this jam's own rubric (`RULES.md`: Ambition and Innovation, Use of Contest Theme, Fun Factor, Gameplay, Visuals, Level Design, Audio — ties re-scored with double-weighted Fun Factor), which already tells you Fun Factor is the rubric's own tiebreaker.

### Cited Findings
- Theme interpretation matters more than technical ambition — "a simple game with a clever theme twist beats a technically impressive game that barely relates to the theme." Complete, simple games outscore incomplete, ambitious ones. — [Stray Spark Studio jam survival guide](https://www.strayspark.studio/blog/game-jam-survival-guide-tools-templates), 2026-04-01
- GMTK 2024 ("Built to Scale") winners spanned literal, metaphorical, and unrelated-word readings of the same theme ("fish scales, climbing"); immediate player enjoyment ("just give the player a good time") outweighed mechanical depth. Puzzle-platformer was the single most common genre in the top 100 — oversaturated but still viable. — [GMTK Substack, "The best games from GMTK Game Jam"](https://gmtk.substack.com/p/the-best-games-from-gmtk-game-jam), 2024-09-04
- GMTK 2023 ("Roles Reversed") favorites mostly *inverted* a familiar genre's core mechanic rather than inventing a new one (tower defense → "tower offence"; stealth → "be spotted"), and judge commentary specifically praised a mid-playthrough mechanical reversal that flips the player's role. Breadth of theme interpretation (the judge sorted favorites into five distinct reading categories) was rewarded over one literal execution repeated well. — [GMTK Substack](https://gmtk.substack.com/p/the-best-games-from-gmtk-game-jam-500) (exact permalink-to-year match unconfirmed — see Gaps), 2023-07-19
- Gradual, per-level introduction of new rules beat front-loaded complexity in two named GMTK 2024 winners. Novelty built on top of a familiar mechanic (one entry added tactics on top of Katamari-style rolling) was specifically called out as impressive — i.e., the safer play is iterating on a known genre, not inventing a new one. — [GMTK Substack](https://gmtk.substack.com/p/the-best-games-from-gmtk-game-jam), 2024-09-04
- A short-countdown-plus-incremental-upgrade loop and a strict 10-instruction Zachtronics-style constraint were both called out as 2026 GMTK favorites — tight self-imposed constraints read as a feature, not a limitation, to commentators. — [Game Industry Library, "My favourite games from GMTK Game Jam 2026"](https://gameindustrylibrary.com/documents/my-favourite-games-from-gmtk-game-jam-2026), 2026-08-08
- One academic interview study of professional/academic jammers found "fun" was the single most spontaneously-raised design value, and that jams are seen as low-stakes environments that specifically reward experimental risk-taking (low cost of failure). — [W. Goddard, "Expect the Unexpected: Designerly Themes for Game Jams"](https://wgoddard.com/assets/papers/expect-the-unexpected-designerly-themes-for-game-jams/expect-the-unexpected-designerly-themes-for-game-jams.pdf) (sourceQuality: primary/academic), undated, cites CHI '14-era work

**Opinion, flagged as such:** given the rubric's own Fun-Factor tiebreaker and the repeated judge commentary above, the single highest-leverage design choice for this entry is probably *take one well-understood, easy-to-build genre and twist its core verb* rather than invent a novel mechanic from scratch — that's a synthesis judgment from the pattern above, not a quoted claim from any one source.

## 2. What's actually buildable solo, in Blueprint, in ~4 days

### Takeaway
Genre choice has a large, previously-quantified effect on jam feasibility. 2D top-down shooters and 2D platformers are repeatedly named as the fastest-to-MVP genres; 3D, RPG, and RTS scope should be treated as a red flag for a solo 4-day entry. Blueprint-only (not C++) is explicitly recommended for Unreal jams specifically because of community/tooling support, not just raw speed.

### Cited Findings
- "Top-down shooters and 2D platformers are most accessible... Western RPGs and RTS games demand the most development time... 3D games require approximately four times longer than 2D equivalents." — [GameDeveloper.com, "Coming up with ideas for a game jam game"](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/coming-up-with-ideas-for-a-game-jam-game), 2015-12-02
- Using C++ in an Unreal Engine jam is risky and slow to start because most community solutions and workflows are Blueprint-specific; Blueprint is "the practical choice for jam speed." The final day of a short jam should be reserved entirely for stabilization/packaging/submission, not new features — "the 5th day is an illusion." Avoid multiplayer unless you already have prior experience with it; it eats disproportionate time for the payoff. — [GameDeveloper.com, "A game jam postmortem: tips for Unreal Engine 4 beginners"](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/programming/a-game-jam-postmortem-tips-for-unreal-engine-4-beginners), 2018-09-06
- A prescribed scope timeline from one jam guide: hours 0–2 for ideation, picking one idea that is buildable, fun within 30 seconds of trying it, and intuitive; then lock the core mechanic alone for the next 6 hours before adding any content on top of it. — [Stray Spark Studio](https://www.strayspark.studio/blog/game-jam-survival-guide-tools-templates), 2026-04-01
- Browser-playable submissions get substantially more plays/ratings than download-only ones — relevant to visibility/outcomes but not a scored criterion in this jam's rubric (packaging here is for Windows/macOS/Android/iOS per `RULES.md`, not browser). — same source

## 3. Theme-flexible mechanic design (so pre-Nov-11 prep actually survives the reveal)

### Takeaway
Three sources converge on the same structural idea even though none cite each other: build the core loop around a *resource* or an *inversion of a known mechanic*, not a theme-specific verb — that gives you a stable system the still-unknown theme can plug into on Nov 11 without a rewrite. This is directly compatible with this jam's own rule that only tooling/MCP/config may be prepared pre-contest, not gameplay itself (`RULES.md`, Rule 7(h)) — the prep work described below is about *design thinking*, not banned pre-built gameplay.

### Cited Findings
- "Verbs are exactly what limitations tend to restrict or reroute. A resource is safer: something that goes up, down, or gets spent, with no opinion yet about how." The author's own jam example: a "focus meter" resource for a Psionic-themed entry let a later-announced "enemies as weapons" constraint drop in as a single conditional branch instead of a rewrite. Proposed test for whether a late theme-twist was genuinely integrated: if deleting its code leaves the original game playing fine with no structural gap, the twist was decorative, not load-bearing. — [dev.to, "Designing a game system that can absorb a constraint you don't know yet"](https://dev.to/fernforge/designing-a-game-system-that-can-absorb-a-constraint-you-dont-know-yet-4fo5), August 10 (year unstated in extracted content)
- Developers can make an existing core mechanic theme-flexible by layering an additional self-imposed constraint on it, rather than inventing a new mechanic per theme — e.g. a shooter where the player can't shoot directly but must reflect bullets with delay-based mechanics. Pre-jam warmups (picking 2–4 concepts against *past* jam themes) are recommended prep, since many resulting concepts transfer across multiple future themes. Avoid your first five theme-derived ideas — other entrants will converge on the same obvious readings. — [GameDeveloper.com, "Coming up with ideas for a game jam game"](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/coming-up-with-ideas-for-a-game-jam-game), 2015-12-02
- One academic interview found at least one professional jammer actively *preferred* theme-light or no-theme jams, finding jam context and social pressure sufficient constraint on their own — and that an organizer's narrowly-specified theme was reported by a participant to measurably narrow the design space explored. This cuts the other way from "theme interpretation matters most" in §1: it's a data point that theme-flexible design isn't just a hedge, some experienced jammers see tight theming as actively limiting. — [W. Goddard paper](https://wgoddard.com/assets/papers/expect-the-unexpected-designerly-themes-for-game-jams/expect-the-unexpected-designerly-themes-for-game-jams.pdf)

## 4. Scope traps (the thing most likely to sink a solo entry)

### Takeaway
This is the best-evidenced section — one quantitative postmortem study and five narrative postmortems all land on the same finding: scope/feature creep is the dominant failure mode in game development generally, and jam-specific advice is just "name your scope cut earlier."

### Cited Findings
- A 2011 analysis of 24 published postmortems (Feb 2008–Jan 2010) found 71% reported scope problems from insufficient time/resources, and scope/feature creep + resource problems were the single largest category (23%) of 68 identified production issues — ahead of team-communication issues (10%) and late critical events (9%). 38% of projects crunched; 38% got schedule extensions (weeks to 17 months, in one case). Core conclusion: failure is overwhelmingly a *process/production management* problem, not a design/art/code-quality one. — [GameDeveloper.com, "Dissecting the postmortem"](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/audio/dissecting-the-postmortem-lessons-learned-from-two-years-of-game-development-self-reportage), 2011-07-08
- A team that discovered a critical scope issue only partway through a jam had to pivot mid-jam — costly specifically because it was caught late. "The sooner you identify a scope issue, the sooner you can remedy it." Successful scope cuts start from what motivates the team about the concept and cut everything else down from there, rather than cutting arbitrarily. — [GameDeveloper.com, "A successful scoping down"](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/a-successful-scoping-down-controlling-expectations-in-a-game-jam), 2023-07-27
- One solo jam postmortem: the dev had to cut scope three separate times to ship anything; "a clear scope can be valuable, but only when I am willing to make drastic cuts to it." Without upfront architectural discipline, the codebase got progressively more chaotic under time pressure. — [arturklemens.itch.io postmortem](https://arturklemens.itch.io/findersweepers/devlog/1096750/finders-sweepers-postmortem), ~2024-10 (approximate)
- Keep scope minimal and resist feature creep; if the stripped-down prototype isn't fun without extra features, it's probably not right for the jam. A single feature addition's *ripple effects* (one power-up meant a new player mechanic, a new enemy type, and new art) is a named, specific scope trap. A team member stuck fighting a non-essential feature for a long stretch should be pulled off it — one blocked person costs disproportionately more in a short jam. — [GameDeveloper.com UE4 postmortem](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/programming/a-game-jam-postmortem-tips-for-unreal-engine-4-beginners), 2018-09-06
- Time constraints themselves are "a powerful motivator" that prevents scope creep by forcing a steady pace — but overambitious jam scope (one cited case: a 14-day jam) took a real physical toll even when the output was good. Standard advice (keep it simple, sleep, use familiar tools) is widely repeated but, by the same author's own account, hard to actually follow. A trivial, complete project (a 3-hour Flappy Bird clone) is itself a validating outcome. — [GameDeveloper.com, "What I learned from doing a game jam every month for a year"](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/what-i-learned-from-doing-a-game-jam-every-month-for-a-, 2021-01-28
